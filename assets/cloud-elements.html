Where do we go as the land crumbles under our feet? How do we learn to grow again, once the tower has fallen? At a time when multiple, overlapping crises—environmental disasters, pandemics, economic recession, the spread of fascist ideologies—cast such long shadows on the choreographies of the everyday, a reflection on the theme of world-endings—and beginnings—feels inevitable. In his book Ideas to Postpone the End of the World, writer and activist Ailton Krenak (2019) notes that at the moment when Portuguese ships landed in the coast of what is now known as Brazil, the worlds of the indigenous peoples who encountered them ended. There is an immeasurable grief that comes with that; a mourning as long as death. Concurrently, the invasion and pillaging of Abya Yala is emblematic of Modernity's inception; the making of the colonial world-system. In a moment where anthropogenic climate emergency presents an existential threat to multiple worlds and worldviews, Krenak asks—how do we navigate change, and how do we postpone this end of the world? How to we learn to make space for multiple worlds and worldviews?

Central to the relationship between contemporary capitalism and world-endings is the concept of "resource". What constitutes a resource is contextual; it is a concept that emerges and is realised within specific and contingent historical, cultural, and geopolitical frameworks. Geographer Philippe Le Billon links the struggle over resources to the colonial project, writing that “[r]esources have provided some of the means and motive of global European power expansion, while also being the focus of inter-state rivalry and strategic denial of access” (2004, p.02). Water, gold, diamonds, oil, copper—all have, at some point or another, and to a greater or lesser extent, been classified as resources.

The repercussions of what Le Billon terms as “resource wars”—conflicts stemming from the struggle for access to these commodities—are vast. In the beginning of the 21st century, the greed for access and control over oil was a key animating factor behind the imperial wars initiated by the United States of America (USA) and its allies in Afghanistan and Iraq. These conflicts changed the political and economic configuration of the world, killing and displacing hundreds of thousands of people. In 1973, right after the coup that killed leftist President Salvador Allende, the military regime of General Augusto Pinochet made it one of its top priorities to grant the USA unrestricted access to the country's copper reserves — which had been nationalised by Allende. Earlier still, at the end of the 17th century, access to gold had been key to the colonial expansion of the Portuguese Empire in the land currently known as Brazil — part of the Portuguese project to maintain economic competitiveness with the British and the Dutch. Human and more-than-human life has, at times, also been classified as a resource.

Animated by the logics of extraction, capture, and circulation, the Regime of the Cloud emerges then not as a rupture from previous configurations of power, but within this colonial continuum. In the book A Decolonial Ecology, Malcolm Ferdinand proposes an outline of what he calls the double fracture of modernity to refer to the separation between what he identifies as the two foundational dimensions of the contemporary planetary crisis. The environmental fracture constructs a hierarchy that places “Man” (an intentionally gendered term) above nature; the colonial fracture sustains a global racial and geopolitical hierarchy that dehumanises and marginalises colonised and racialised peoples. These two fractures, which are rarely addressed together, create a deep epistemic and political divide that obscures how colonial domination and ecological devastation are co-constitutive processes rooted in the same modern, imperial project.

The Regime of the Cloud materialises this double fracture in its physical infrastructures and demands, as well as in the hierarchies and modes of doing it establishes. The environmental fracture reveals itself across cables laid across ocean floors, tracing the outlines of colonial shipping routes and the maritime corridors of the transatlantic slave trade—routes through which bodies were once transported, now replaced by data packets and algorithmic signals. It appears in the silica extracted from coastal sands, pulverised and purified to feed the insatiable demand for semiconductors; in the niobium ore torn from Indigenous lands in Brazil, a mineral deemed strategic not for its utility to local communities, but for its role in the functioning of Western military and digital systems. It echoes in the deserts of Chile, where lithium is siphoned from ancestral salt flats under the guise of green transition, and in the deep boreholes drilled for cooling data centres, where freshwater is quietly rerouted away from farms and households to keep the cloud from overheating.

The colonial fracture determines who will be the ones to carry the burden of this idea of progress and development—a burden that manifests itself in a myriad of affective and physical spaces. It is carried in the repetitive, traumatising labour of content moderators in Kenya, paid minimum wage and offered no psychological support whilst exposed to the most toxic content on the internet. It is carried in the forced displacement of the Anacé people in Ceará, Brazil, whose ancestral lands are now at the centre of a supposed “digital innovation” project encompassing the construction of a new, sprawling data centre—a collaboration between TikTok and “green" energy provider Casa dos Ventos. It is carried by the exploited click-workers in the Philippines, whose labour trains the AI systems that power global platforms whilst leaving them in precarity and invisibility. And it is carried, too, by the people of Gaza—whose every movement is tracked, catalogued, and targeted through drone and surveillance technologies reliant on cloud infrastructures.

To help render visible how the Cloud both reproduces and extends these dynamics, this project approaches the Cloud through its connection to three elements: Earth, Water, and Air. These elements provide a structure to trace the Cloud's technoecological operations across space and scale.

* here, we follow the minerals that make the Cloud possible—niobium, lithium, silica—and the territories rendered sacrificial zones in the process. Here, the double fracture becomes evident: nature becomes a warehouse; communities are no longer sovereign, but obstructive. In Brazil, the presence of niobium in Indigenous territories such as Raposa Serra do Sol brings new waves of militarisation and land conflict as the mineral becomes increasingly strategic for technologies like drones and satellites.

* Water (Distribution): Submarine cables and cooling systems become vectors of violence. The logics of speed and latency reroute landscapes and labor alike, mapping colonial routes of circulation onto a supposedly borderless internet. The installation of TikTok's data center in Ceará, Brazil—on lands previously occupied by the Anacé people—reveals how these infrastructures displace communities while claiming to bring progress and investment.

* Air (Application): Here we find drones, banking apps, targeted ads. The Cloud modulates desire, governs movement, profiles subjects. In Brazil, the state-developed PIX payment system represents an attempt at infrastructural sovereignty—offering a platform for financial transactions that sidesteps private banking monopolies and international fintech platforms. Yet even here, we must ask: who controls the rails? Who stores the data? What logics govern the interface?
