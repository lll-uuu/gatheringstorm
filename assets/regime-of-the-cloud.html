We float amongst clouds, light, weightless, limbs like feathers. The soft mist caresses our skin, enters our noses and mouths, coats our tongues, fills our lungs.

In meteorology, a cloud is often defined as an aerosol composed by visible yet minute particles of water, ice crystals, and other assorted elements, suspended somewhere above the Earth's surface. Their composition and form can vary across time and space, reflecting the conditions under which they are formed. Clouds regulate temperature; they are able to trap heat on the surface of the Earth, and to dissipate it by reflecting solar energy back into the outer atmosphere. They carry rain to parched soils, distributing water across the entire surface of the planet and offering fundamental nourishment to all beings. Conversely, clouds can also be harbingers or death. They can carry the ashes of a forest fire across thousands of kilometres, in a morbid planetary funeral; they can unleash storms of acid rain, mass death raining from above; cloud formation can be affected by CO2 levels, writing the narrative of our climate catastrophe across the skies. Their presence, although constant, is mostly silent, muted; an idle engine quietly working in the backdrop of Earthly rhythms.

The amorphous instability of clouds lends itself easily to metaphor. Whilst the clouds that float above our heads are often referred to in plural form, there is another type of amorphous mass that is, too, responsible for shaping fundamental aspects of contemporary life. The Cloud, often used in the singular form, hovers just as persistently over our lives—this one not meteorological, but computational. This Cloud, too, is composed of suspended, atomised particles: lines of code, electrical pulses, server heat, ocean-floor cables, extraction maps, packet routes. It, too, reflects, traps, and redistributes energy—although this time not solar, but economic, political, and affective. The Cloud presents itself as an ethereal non-space: limitless, seamless, quietly working in the background to smooth out the jagged edges of daily life. Its haze can be deceiving; with eyes covered by the mist of convenience and speed, the sharp edges of a dense and brutal architecture of power built on circuits of domination and extraction become nothing more than shadows lurking at the edge of perception.

In the book A Prehistory of the Cloud, author Tung-Hui Hu describes the Cloud as a “resource-intensive, extractive technology that converts water and electricity into computational power, leaving a sizeable amount of environmental damage that it then displaces from sight.” (2015, p. 146). Approaching the Cloud as a technology, however, obscures the foundational role it has acquired in how power is currently distributed, exercised, and resisted. We start, thus, by proposing a yet-incomplete, working outline of what we call the Regime of the Cloud: a techno-social amalgamate of tools, infrastructures, devices and modes of doing that produces harm to all forms of life.

This regime establishes modes of governance that structure and regulate behaviours, reconfiguring our perceptions of subjects, infrastructures, and territories along colonial and capitalist hierarchies. It unfurls across data centers and tax shelters, moves through cables that cross ocean floors and sensors carried in our pockets and in our palms, it stretches from mines at the bowels of the earth to satellites crowing the stratosphere. What we call the Regime of the Cloud is a techno-ecological system that coalesces through the articulation of atomised material-semiotic actors—visible and present, yet often operating in the background of our daily lives. It reconfigures the conditions under which subjects, infrastructures, and territories are made legible, governable, and therefore valuable; a “topography or architecture of our own desire” (Hu 2015, p.XVII). The Regime of the Cloud coalesces in legal and financial grey zones where jurisdiction is partial, and accountability elusive; it flows through tax havens and fibre-optic territories, network diagrams as “enclaves isolated by virtualization technology—and, at the border between the public Internet and a corporation or user's private data” (ibid. p. 91).

In his historical outline, Hu notes that “the twin ideas of territoriality and 'the right to kill' exist as an unassimilated layer within contemporary digital culture, evidence that some version or mutation of sovereign power is present in the cloud” (ibid., p.94, my emphasis). Indeed, big tech companies are implicated in practices that provoke deep, sometimes irreparable harm to communities and landscapes—from their involvement with mining and the depletion of natural resources, to their enthusiastic leadership in the development of military technologies that cause immense suffering and are instrumental to sustaining colonial order. The question of sovereignty reveals, as Hu argues, a conceptual continuity between Cloud computing and older formations of power. Michel Foucault defines sovereign power as the right to seize—assets, labor, life itself—while biopower, emerging with modernity, shifts the focus toward managing life through the regulation of health, birth, and productivity. No longer about killing outright, power becomes the capacity to “foster life or disallow it to the point of death.” Giorgio Agamben, however, argues that biopolitics is not a rupture but a continuation of Western political traditions, rooted in the exclusion of bare life—bodies stripped of rights, suspended in a zone of exception where law does not apply and violence can be enacted without consequence.

Virtualised, obfuscated and abstracted in the grey zones where the power of the State meets the power of capital, Cloud computing models enact the logic of sovereignty not through explicit declarations of death, but through algorithmic decisions: whose data is protected, whose body is targeted, whose movement is flagged, whose existence is archived or deleted. Under the haze and the mist, the architecture of sovereign power might appear as a neutral technical system. It is through the Cloud, however, that Big Tech companies are able to buy and sell data, train AI agents, and fine-tune advertisements to more effectively target their users; it is also through the Cloud that military technologies, from drones to missiles, are operated. On a symbolic level, the Cloud plays a key conceptual role: that of sustaining the capitalist illusion that infinite growth is both sustainable and achievable. The profits generated through this system—measured in capital accummulation—are funnelled to a narrow set of corporations and individuals; its disasters—economic, environmental, political, cultural—are, in turn, displaced to those made most vulnerable under a system of colonial continuities.

Here, we are drawn to Achille Mbembe's outline necropolitics. For Mbembe, the colony is the original state of exception. The plantation, the ghetto, the occupied zone; these are sites where the regulation of life and the production of death become indistinguishable. In the techno-ecological space of the Cloud, infrastructures of care, extraction, surveillance, service, health, and targeting all overlap. The drone, the database, the content moderation system act as contemporary theatres of necropolitical designs, where the Regime of the Cloud governs not through the spectacle of violence, but through slow, routinised abstraction. Behind in the haze, the architecture of power that sustains the Cloud reveals itself not as a site of biopolitical governance, but of necropolitical sorting.

To speak of the cloud, then, is to speak of a terrain, an infrastructural form, a space of sharp edges concealed in a language of abstraction and convenience. The Regime of the Cloud emerges as a technoecological space: an unstable, shifting, haunted constellation of bodies, systems, laws, and territories that coagulate around servers and servers, protocols, experiences, logics and user interfaces. In this sense, the cloud is not merely a technical infrastructure but a potent site of worlding—an entangled process through which ways of being and ways of making worlds continuously shape one another. Drawing on Anne-Marie Willis' notion of ontological designing, we can understand the cloud as actively participating in this co-constitutive relation: it configures subjectivities, reorders relations to time, space, and matter, and scripts behaviours at both individual, societal, and planetary scales. The Regime of the Cloud engages in worlding by embedding its protocols into daily life, producing norms and rhythms that feel natural, inevitable, and even desirable—and in so doing, it reorganises the conditions of existence under extractive, racialised, and imperial goals.
